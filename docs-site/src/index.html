Documentation

Notes on how to make a CROW Card, what we mean by terms and categories, definitions for potential values, and so on. Choosing where to build your community's online space? See Choosing a platform.

Getting started

The MAKE feature assumes you've already set up (or at least selected) a system for your community to sit within. If you haven't, have a look at Choosing a platform: an overview of some common systems communities use, alongside what we think is useful to know (because it can shape how much autonomy or power your community has!): whether the software is open source, whether you can host an instance of it yourself, what structural model it follows, and what costs you should expect.

By community, we take a pretty broad view: a group that engages in and enforces some kind of collective decision-making, has a shared (digital) space, has boundaries for membership, and some kind of shared purpose. However, this also excludes some informal or cultural communities we often colloquially refer to, e.g., a distinct subset of users on Bluesky. This is not to say those groups can't use the CROW Card Maker! We presume that there just wouldn't be a clear place to post the Card itself, nor clear mechanisms to enforce the values and practices that are stated on it.

Examples.

A Reddit subreddit qualifies: moderators set rules, and the community experiences them. A Facebook Group qualifies too, but note that Facebook can override or dissolve it unilaterally, which limits sovereignty. Likewise, Bonfire instances and other groups on community-oriented platform software generally count as communities (and in the case of Bonfire, some features may even result in some nested communities).

A TikTok account or just a social media feed does not qualify: there is no community governance surface, only an audience and an algorithm.

Some emergent systems also create some interesting new cases of online community: labeling services like Blacksky (built on AT Protocol) govern a normative filter, deciding whose content is visible to people who opt in, which is a real form of collective decision-making (but not for a shared venue in the traditional sense).

If your community is on a Mastodon server, you can start from its link: set-up fills in what the server shares publicly (its name, description, size, platform, how people join and its rules), for you to check.

Getting started is simple. You can create a new Card by going to MAKE and entering some basic information about your community:

  • name
  • community type - the kind of group, e.g. a workplace, a neighborhood, a support group. This shapes what's suggested to you.
  • format - the kind of online space, e.g. a chat group or a forum. This suggests which modules you'll want to fill out. You can change both the format and the module selection.
  • size and link
  • a short description: its purpose and its culture
  • keywords - we recommend giving at least three
  • where you'd like your community to be on three scales (from 1 to 7): how participatory, transparent and hierarchical (see the three scales). Each shows its trade-offs. These are only shown publicly if you share your full Card.
  • values - up to five; some are suggested, from your community type, format, description and keywords, and where you'd like to be

Then pick the modules your Card will cover (Infrastructure, Membership, Rules, Processes, Federation, or your own), and fill them in at your own pace.

Important!

When you create a new Card, the app will give you an id and secret key. Save it somewhere! You will only get this once and you can use it to edit/update your Card. You can also choose to have this browser remember your Card; Forget in the editor clears it from the device.

You may want to think about how your CROW Card will be filled out or updated. For example, some communities may want to convene town halls to have a participatory discussion about their governance practices, using the CROW Card app as an occasion to reflect, iterate, and document. Others might be simply run by one person who fills it out unilaterally. It will depend on your community (however, we do encourage using it as an excuse to convene)!

We do run workshops to help communities fill out the CROW Card. Reach out!

Using the editor

A few things that work the same way throughout the CROW Card Maker:

  • Saving is automatic. Your answers save as you go; the status in the sidebar says when they last did. Save saves right away.
  • Modules can be switched on and off under Basics → Modules. Switching one off hides it, but keeps what you've filled in, in case you switch it back on.
  • Suggested and See also chips mark values and approaches that may suit your community, from what you said in Basics: its community type, format, description and keywords, where you'd like to be on the three scales, its size, and the values you selected. Each says why, including anything that counts against it. At most five values are Suggested. A few options say why they're generally not a fit for groups like yours. Nothing is ever selected for you.
  • More like this / Try something new. When you select an approach or a value, a row under your choices offers similar ones, and a few different ones to consider; click one to add it.
  • Suggestions appear at the top of a module when something you entered elsewhere affects it, e.g. choosing a federated platform suggests the Federation module. If one appears out of sight, a small "New suggestion" notice points to it.
  • Fill in from your link. If your community is on a Mastodon server, enter its address as your link and select "Fill in from this link": the name, description, size, platform, how people join, rules and any public block list are offered for you to pick from. More platforms to come.
  • Pre-filling. Choosing a platform you know fills in its details and the costs that are always or never paid on that kind of platform. Costs that are only sometimes paid are left for you to answer, with what's usual shown beside each as a guide. Change anything that's different for you.
  • Notes on what you choose. Your choices show as tags; click one to add a note about how it works in your community. Tags with a note get a small asterisk.
  • Changing your mind is safe. If you unselect something, change a Yes to a No, or switch a field to another kind, what you'd entered for it is set aside rather than lost: switch back, even another day, and it's there again. Set-aside answers aren't part of your Card or its exports.
  • Steps. Conflict management and Federation's response ladder are put in order by dragging: approaches on the same step happen in parallel. In conflict management you can also mark your preferred (primary) ways.
  • Your community structure (how decisions get made) is one list: you set it in Membership, and adjust it, with notes, for changing the rules, maintenance and moderation in Processes.
  • Laws to know. Under Infrastructure → Locations, you can open a page of landmark laws for where your servers, members and admins are, and save it as a PDF. It's an informational starting point, not legal advice.
  • Your own modules. Add a module for anything the built-in ones don't cover, with fields for written answers, checkboxes, radio buttons, or a scale (1–5, 1–7 or 0–10).
  • Publish. On the Export page, publish your Card at a link to share, choosing how much it shows (Minimal, Foggy, Misty or Full). Each publish is a new version; your draft stays private until you publish changes. You can unpublish at any time.
  • Export. Download your Card as raw data (JSON) or as documentation (Markdown). Only Basics and the modules you use are included.
  • Reset clears the modules you choose, or starts the whole Card over (it keeps its id and secret).

Publishing

On the Export page you can publish your Card at a link to share. Your Card stays a private draft: readers see the version you published, until you publish your edits as a new version. You choose how much readers see, and who can find it, and you can change either at any time without publishing again.

ViewWhat readers seeCan be used as a starting point

Part by part:

What a view leaves out isn't just hidden: it isn't sent to readers at all. Earlier versions are shown in the same view.

Who can find it

  • Unlisted: only people you give the link to can open it.
  • Listed: it will also appear in the Library, for anyone to find. The Library is coming soon: until then, listed Cards can also only be opened by link.

Contact and credit

Publishing needs a contact email. Only the CROW team sees it, to contact you about your Card; it's never shown publicly. Your name and organization are optional, and shown on the published Card only if you select them.

Versions and unpublishing

  • Each time you publish your edits, the Card gets a new version (v1, v2, …), with an optional note on what changed. Readers can look back at earlier versions.
  • Unpublish takes the Card down: its link stops working. Your published versions are kept privately, so publishing again carries on from them.
  • Unpublish and delete history also deletes every published version, so publishing again starts at v1.

Reading a published Card

  • Above the Card: its version, when it was published, which view it's shown in (with what that view shares in the "i"), and any credit. Under it: earlier versions, where it was forked from, and buttons to share its link, fork it, and (in the Full view) download it.
  • The top: the community's name (a link to it, when given), its community type, format and size, and where it is (hover a place to see whether it's where the servers, members or admins are).
  • Modules each sit in a light outline. Whatever wasn't filled in is left out.
  • Notes sit behind a small asterisk: hover it, tab to it or tap it to read the note. Long notes are cut short; the whole note is in the raw data download.
  • Other things with more to say show it the same way, in a bubble, and turn orange when you point at them: the joining icons, tools, protocols and the scales.
  • The three scales are drawn as icons with a column of squares, filled to what the community's choices add up to. In the Full view, the square outlined in dots is where the community would like to be. See the three scales.
  • A star marks a primary step in conflict management.

Open source (OSI)

The Open Source Initiative (OSI) defines open source software as software whose license grants freedoms to use, study, modify, and distribute. A platform is labeled open source when its software is released under an OSI-approved license. A few cases worth noting:

  • Telegram — client apps are open source (GPLv3), but the server is proprietary and not published. Since the server is the operative component for a platform, Telegram is classified as not open source.
  • Signal — both client and server are AGPLv3, so it is open source. However, Signal controls the central infrastructure and the server is not practically self-hostable to interoperate with the main network, hence Self-hostable = No.
  • Fandom — built on a proprietary fork of MediaWiki. Although MediaWiki itself is open source (GPLv2+), Fandom's modifications are not published under an OSI-compatible license.
  • Wikipedia — the MediaWiki software is open source (GPLv2+), but Wikipedia itself is hosted exclusively by the Wikimedia Foundation. The software label is open source; the platform is not self-hostable as Wikipedia.

Expected costs

On a Card, each cost is answered Yes or No: whether your community pays for it, in money or time. What's usual is worked out from each platform's type rather than platform by platform, and the editor shows it beside each cost as a guide. The main cost drivers are infrastructure ownership (do you host it?) and content type (does it generate heavy media?). Everything else — admin labor, third-party tools — applies broadly regardless of platform choice and depends more on your community's size and activity.

  • Self-hosting means you bear hosting, domain, email delivery, backups, and potentially managed hosting costs. Proprietary or institutionally-hosted platforms mean you bear none of these.
  • Media-heavy platforms (PeerTube, Pixelfed) add significant storage and delivery costs on top of the standard self-hosting baseline.
  • Protocol-native clients (Nostr, AT Protocol) have costs that only arise if you choose to run your own relay or Personal Data Server — most community runners won't.
  • Admin and moderation labor is the one cost that cuts across all platform types, because it follows community size and culture rather than technical architecture.

What the words mean:

ValueMeaning

All values reflect what a community organizer or operator typically pays — not the platform company. For corporate / proprietary platforms, that's what a group admin or community manager pays. For self-hosted platforms, what an instance operator pays. For protocol client rows (Nostr, AT Protocol), what running your own relay or PDS costs; most community runners on these platforms will not incur infrastructure costs.

Most platforms take what's usual from their platform type. A handful have overrides where the type's isn't accurate for that platform — for example, Discourse requires email delivery for notifications, and Mailman is itself an email delivery system.

Structure: the three scales

A community's structure is how decisions get made among its members: the decision-making approaches it selects (described under Processes → Decision-making). Each approach, joining tier, way of joining and conflict approach sits somewhere on three scales, from 1 to 7; a Card's score on each is the average of its choices. In Basics, communities also say where they'd like to be.

Processes

How a community makes decisions, handles conflict, and communicates. The decision-making and conflict management approaches are from the CommunityRule project by Metagov.

Decision-making

The approaches a community selects describe its general structure: how decisions get made (set in Membership). In Processes, the same list is adjusted, with notes, for three kinds of decisions in particular: changing the rules, maintenance and moderation. A community typically picks a few, often different approaches for different kinds of decisions (e.g., lazy consensus for daily operations, full consensus for governance changes).

ApproachDescription

Conflict management

Approaches for handling conflicts between members. Communities put the ones they use in steps, in the order they'd try them (first, escalation, last resort); several on the same step happen in parallel, and preferred ways can be marked primary.
